24 × 42-Foot Modern Linear Home with a Daylit Side Gallery

A compact two-bedroom home concept with a flexible study, a long side gallery, connected living and dining spaces, a practical service zone and a simple mono-pitch roof silhouette.

24 × 42 ftNominal building grid
1,008 sq ftGrid area before wall adjustments
2 + 1Bedrooms plus flexible study
4 ftNominal side-gallery module
Read the dimensions carefully: The room schedule is a preliminary planning allocation that adds up to the 24 × 42-foot grid before wall thickness, columns, service ducts and finish build-ups are deducted. The labelled modules are not verified clear internal dimensions. The diagrams do not establish structure, foundations, roof framing, code compliance or construction set-out. A qualified local architect or building designer and the relevant engineers must adapt and check the design for the actual land, soil, climate, utilities and approval requirements.

1. The design brief: a long, narrow modern home

This concept is intended for a relatively narrow plot where a simple rectangular building is easier to organise than a house with several projecting wings. Rather than placing a central open courtyard in the middle of the plan, it uses a four-foot side gallery as a continuous circulation and daylight zone. The gallery gives the bedrooms, bathroom, study and kitchen a clear route to the entrance and provides a place for a slim console, wall art or shallow storage where the final clear width allows.

The front half contains the living and dining spaces. The kitchen sits directly behind the dining zone so that meals can move between the two without crossing the private rooms. A flexible study or occasional guest room sits behind the living area. At the rear, the main bedroom and a compact second bedroom share a bathroom reached from the gallery. The arrangement is deliberately modest: there is no garage, large internal courtyard or ensuite bathroom in this first-stage concept.

The modern character comes from proportion and shade rather than decorative complexity: a low mono-pitch roof, a deep but restrained entry canopy, simple wall planes, a screened side gallery, larger openings to the more private garden side and a limited palette of light render, timber-look accents and dark window frames. These are visual choices, not a claim that one façade treatment will suit every climate or budget.

2. Overall dimensions and room schedule

The nominal main grid is 24 feet wide by 42 feet deep, giving 1,008 square feet before wall adjustments. A separate 6 × 8-foot covered entry porch could add approximately 48 square feet if the site and local rules allow it; it is not included in the 1,008-square-foot grid. The plan labels the front side at the bottom of the diagrams only to make the drawings readable. Rotate the arrangement to suit the actual road, sun path, prevailing wind, views and neighbouring privacy conditions.

3. Conceptual 2D floor plan

The living and dining spaces sit at the front. A wide opening links the living room to the flexible room behind it, while the dining area connects to the kitchen. This creates two short routes for daily use without forcing people to walk through a bedroom. The gallery runs along the east side of the diagram and gives access to the kitchen, study, bedrooms and bathroom. The main entrance is shown near the front dining zone, with the gallery acting as the internal distribution route.

The kitchen is kept close to the bathroom and laundry service side to make early plumbing coordination easier to study. This does not determine the final pipe route or drain fall; those depend on the actual site levels, fixture positions and local service requirements. The bathroom is reached from the gallery rather than through a bedroom, so it can serve the household and visitors.

4. Doors, windows and circulation

Indicative door locations

  • Main entrance: shown at the front dining zone. A covered 6 × 8-foot porch may be considered outside the main grid, subject to site access and boundary conditions.
  • Gallery access: a connecting door links the dining space to the side gallery so the gallery can distribute movement to the private rooms.
  • Bedrooms: the main and guest bedrooms open from the gallery, keeping the sleeping areas separate from the living room.
  • Bathroom: the bathroom door opens from the gallery. A pocket or outward-opening door may reduce fixture conflicts, but the selected hardware and clear opening need local review.
  • Kitchen and study: each has a door or opening to the gallery; the kitchen also has a direct connection to dining.

Window and daylight strategy

The front façade uses separate living and dining windows. The west-side openings bring daylight into the living room, flexible room and main bedroom. A rear window serves the guest room, while the bathroom receives a high-level privacy opening. The side gallery can use high-level windows or screened openings to bring light into the circulation strip without exposing bedroom doors directly to neighbouring views.

Window sizes and positions should be adjusted after checking the actual sun path, rain direction, prevailing breeze, nearby buildings, privacy and security. Larger glazing is not automatically better: it can increase heat gain, glare, cleaning and replacement costs. External shading, operable louvres, insect screens and suitable glass should be selected for the local climate.

Circulation checks before fixing furniture

  • Draw the actual bed, wardrobe, sofa, dining table, refrigerator and kitchen appliance footprints to scale.
  • Check that every door can open without striking a bed, cupboard, sanitary fitting or another door.
  • Keep the gallery free of deep furniture; a shallow wall shelf is easier to accommodate than a full-depth cabinet.
  • Test the dining chairs in their pulled-out position, not only when tucked under the table.
  • Confirm that the guest room can be used with the chosen bed and wardrobe rather than relying on the nominal room label alone.

5. Furnished 3D / axonometric view

The axonometric view removes the roof so the room sequence can be understood at a glance. The main bedroom and guest room occupy the rear zone. The study and kitchen form the middle zone, and the living and dining spaces sit toward the entrance. The long gallery is visible as a continuous strip beside the rooms. Furniture blocks are illustrative footprints only; their dimensions must be replaced with the actual products selected by the household.

For a more detailed 3D model, the designer should use the same wall positions, opening locations, ceiling heights, roof edge and furniture schedule as the final measured 2D drawing. A 3D model that changes the location of a window or moves a bathroom wall is a different design, not a matching view of this plan.

6. Mono-pitch roof concept and rainwater planning

A single-plane mono-pitch roof gives the exterior a clean contemporary profile and avoids the valleys that can occur in more complicated roof forms. In this concept, the high edge is on the west and the roof falls toward a continuous east-side gutter. A clerestory band below the high edge can bring controlled daylight into selected spaces if the final wall and roof section allows it.

The roof diagram is not a structural design. The required pitch, span, framing material, member sizes, bracing, connections, wind uplift resistance, corrosion protection, insulation, condensation control, flashing, gutter capacity, overflow path and downpipe positions must be calculated and detailed by qualified professionals for the actual site. A low-slope roof can be particularly sensitive to workmanship and drainage maintenance, so the roof covering manufacturer's requirements and local rainfall conditions matter.

7. Front, side and rear exterior concepts

Front elevation

The front façade is designed around a simple window rhythm: a wider living-room opening, a more controlled dining-room window and a sheltered entry. A timber-look screen or textured panel can mark the entrance without adding several different materials. The roof edge should provide appropriate shade and rain protection after the local sun and weather are studied.

West side elevation

The west side can use carefully shaded windows for the living room, study and main bedroom. If this side receives strong afternoon sun, external fins, a veranda, planting or reduced glazing may be preferable to large unshaded windows. The high roof edge and clerestory concept should be coordinated with privacy and heat control.

Rear elevation

The rear wall has openings for the main bedroom and guest room, with a smaller high-level bathroom window. The final façade should preserve privacy from adjacent properties and allow safe maintenance access. The rear service area can include a modest drying or planting strip if the plot has enough space.

East gallery elevation

The east side is the gallery face. A combination of high-level windows, a screened side door and a durable lower wall can make this long elevation feel intentional rather than like a blank corridor wall. Keep the gallery clear of rainwater discharge and provide a safe route for cleaning the windows and gutter.

8. Interior character and finish selection

A restrained palette can help a narrow home feel coherent. Consider warm white or pale neutral walls, a matte easy-care floor finish, timber-look joinery for selected storage, and a limited dark finish for window frames and hardware. Use a more durable, moisture-appropriate finish in the kitchen and bathroom than in dry living areas. A small number of repeated materials is usually easier to maintain than a different finish in every room.

In the living room, keep the sofa and media wall proportionate to the 12 × 14-foot module and avoid blocking the front window. In the dining area, a narrow rectangular table may preserve movement between the entrance and kitchen. In the flex room, built-in shelving can use the wall height without taking up the centre of the floor. In the main bedroom, position wardrobes so that drawers and doors can open without blocking the bed route.

The kitchen can use a one-wall or compact galley arrangement. Put the sink, preparation surface and cooking area in a logical sequence, then confirm appliance ventilation, refrigerator door swing, worktop depth and safe clearances with the kitchen supplier. The laundry cupboard shown within the kitchen should have appropriate ventilation, drainage, power and access for maintenance.

9. Electrical, lighting and plumbing coordination

Electrical and lighting considerations

  • Plan general lighting for the living, dining, kitchen, gallery and bedrooms separately so the household can use only the zones needed.
  • Use task lighting at the kitchen worktop, study desk, bathroom mirror and wardrobe area.
  • Locate switches where they can be reached naturally from each room entrance, and check that door swings do not hide them.
  • Allow for refrigerator, cooker, extractor, laundry equipment, water heating, fans, air-conditioning if required, internet equipment and exterior lighting.
  • Coordinate smoke or heat detection, protective devices, earthing, cable routes and load calculations with a licensed local electrical professional.

Plumbing and drainage considerations

The kitchen, laundry cupboard and bathroom are grouped toward the east service side to make a compact service strategy possible. This is only a planning intention. A plumber or building-services designer must determine pipe sizes, gradients, venting, access panels, hot-water routes, cleanouts, rainwater disposal and the connection to the actual site services. Avoid burying valves or joints where they cannot be reached for maintenance.

10. Construction sequence: from site checks to finishing

  1. Site information: confirm boundaries, levels, access, easements, soil conditions, existing services, drainage paths, sun and neighbouring openings before fixing the footprint.
  2. Design development: have the room plan, sections, elevations, door and window schedule, roof details and service layouts coordinated by the appropriate professionals.
  3. Setting out and foundations: set out the approved footprint and build the foundation system specified by the structural engineer for the actual ground conditions.
  4. Floor base and damp protection: complete the specified sub-base, moisture protection, service sleeves and floor construction before finishes are installed.
  5. Walls and openings: construct the selected structural system and wall infill, checking openings, lintels, wall ties and movement details against the issued drawings.
  6. Roof and weatherproofing: install the engineered roof framing, roof covering, insulation, flashings, gutters and downpipes; water-test the completed drainage arrangement where appropriate.
  7. First-fix services: coordinate electrical conduits, plumbing, drainage, ventilation and any cooling routes before closing walls and ceilings.
  8. Windows, doors and finishes: install weather-sealed openings, wet-area waterproofing, floor and wall finishes, joinery, sanitary fittings and paint systems.
  9. Testing and handover: check electrical protection, water pressure, drainage, ventilation, door operation, roof leaks and maintenance access with the relevant professionals.

11. Materials and an approximate cost-planning guide

No reliable total price can be stated from a concept drawing alone. The final cost depends on country and region, site access, ground conditions, structural system, labour, material specifications, taxes, professional fees, approvals, utility connections and the date of purchase. Prices also change over time. Treat the following as a scope checklist, not a quotation or a fixed cost estimate.

13. Limitations and professional review

This article is a design exploration to help a household discuss priorities. It is not a signed architectural drawing, structural calculation, site survey, bill of quantities, electrical design, plumbing design, permit submission or legal/code-compliance statement. The nominal modules do not account for all wall build-ups, columns, ducts, construction tolerances or furniture variations. The mono-pitch roof is shown only as a visual form.

Before construction, ask qualified local professionals to verify the plot and boundary information, planning and approval requirements, accessibility and fire-safety provisions, structural system, foundation design, wind and rainfall conditions, roof drainage, electrical loads, plumbing and sanitation, ventilation, waterproofing and material suitability. The actual orientation and openings should be adapted to the site rather than copied blindly from the diagram.

Frequently asked questions

Is this a ready-to-build 24 × 42-foot plan?

No. It is a preliminary concept with nominal planning modules. A local design professional must develop measured drawings and coordinate the structure, services and approvals.

Can the flex room become a third bedroom?

Potentially, but the household should test privacy, daylight, ventilation, storage, door access and the required furniture. A local professional must check any applicable room-size and opening requirements before that change is adopted.

Can the mono-pitch roof be replaced with a flat roof?

The exterior style can be reconsidered, but a flat or low-slope roof has different drainage, waterproofing, insulation and structural requirements. It should be redesigned rather than treated as a simple finish change.

Does the 1,008-square-foot figure include the porch?

No. It is the nominal 24 × 42-foot main grid. A separate 6 × 8-foot porch would add approximately 48 square feet of covered area if it is built and counted as proposed.

How much will the house cost?

The concept does not support a dependable price. Use a measured professional drawing set, a clear finish schedule and current local quotations to prepare an approximate project budget.

Design takeaway: The main idea is not the roof shape alone. It is the combination of a straightforward rectangular footprint, a continuous side gallery, a flexible middle room and a kitchen placed beside dining. Use the concept as a conversation starter, then have it adapted to the real site and household needs.
